# Caroline Hoff

I used to think math was mostly a race. As many young students do, in elementary school we did “minute math,” and I remember the specific kind of stress it gave me: the feeling that my ability was being measured by speed. I could do the problems, I had been adding and multiplying for years, but not fast enough to feel safe. I had an urge to finish before I even understood what I was doing. Even today, a lot of my relationship with math has been about time: whether I have enough of it, whether I’m using it well, and whether taking longer means I don’t belong or am not useful in the field. By the time I got to high school, I was placed on the accelerated track. What was supposed to be an exciting opportunity began to feel a bit like a punishment as I watched girls disappear from my classes until I found myself in rooms that were almost entirely boys. This punishment also became a banishment from the front row and a habit of leaving my hand down when I had a question so I wouldn’t slow down the fast-paced class. This experience shifted when I ended up in a course with Mrs. Herabat, a former engineer who commanded the room. No side chatter or performative confidence, just a class that actually did the work. She spoke about her experiences managing infrastructure in exclusively male spaces, and how that experience pushed her toward teaching, because she wanted math to feel more available to more people. As the only girl in her class, I felt a strange kind of pressure to not waste the opportunity she described. 

She also taught in a way that exposed one of my weak spots. She expected memorization for exams; we’d never be given the unit circle on a test, we were expected to have it in our brains. At some point I admitted to her that brute memorization didn’t work for me. Instead of sympathy or a shrug, she said, “Then you must not be approaching it the right way. There is always a way to do anything that will work for you.” I think about that advice more than I think about any trigonometric identity. It gave me a direction to stop treating the standard way as the only way. That idea has followed me through the rest of my math education: I don’t always get things right away, but I’ll stay after and work on my own until it clicks. My path isn’t a straight climb, it’s switchbacks. I double back when I need to and try not to apologize for taking a longer route. 

I was excited to find myself in a linear algebra classroom during my first semester of college, and even more excited when it made me confident enough to pursue a math major. It made math feel connected to the world: eigenvalues and eigenvectors showing up in ranking and search made math feel like useful ideas with real consequences. Two semesters later, however, multivariable calculus shook that confidence. I felt unmoored, mostly because everything lived in higher dimensions that I struggled to draw or even picture. The material felt untethered, like it was happening in space, not on any terrain I could walk on. At the same time, I was taking di!erential equations, and the contrast was immediate. Di!erential equations felt tied to real processes–cooling and heating, fluid flow, systems changing over time–so even when the math was di"cult, I could picture what it was trying to describe. That connection pushed me toward the applied math track: math that stays anchored to something I can understand. 

Still, I didn’t want to treat multivariable calculus as a closed door. Two semesters later, I audited it again. The biggest di!erence wasn’t that it became easy, it didn’t, but that I wasn’t time-pressed anymore. I had a whole semester to sit with the ideas and take the switchbacks without the pressure of constant evaluation. I stopped equating slow with incapable and proved to myself that I would come back. That has reshaped what “useful” means to me in math. I used to think usefulness was quick understanding, quick solutions, quick confidence. Now I think it can also mean being careful and thorough, not rushing past confusion, and being able to explain ideas clearly to anyone. A lot of my imposter syndrome has come from reading math writing that feels like it assumes you already know the trail, which steps are safe to skip, and how to overtake its obstacles.
